# Engineering

The project did not begin as a pipeline. It began as an intention to train a predictor, and the first thing the work established was that this intention was wrong. What follows is the account of how that was discovered, what replaced it, and what the replacement forced us to decide — because almost none of the architecture was chosen in advance. It was arrived at, one measurement at a time, and the sequence of arrivals is the engineering.

## The constraint was data, not model capacity

We set out to train a neural network that would predict peptide activity in the three-to-thirty-residue window, and spent the first phase assembling labelled sets that had published support behind them: antioxidant activity first, about eighteen hundred sequences, then every annotated peptide we could collect, just over a million. The survey that followed failed in both directions at once, and the symmetry of the failure is what made it decisive. For the properties with enough labelled data — antioxidant above all — many predictors already exist, so a further model would have added a duplicate rather than a contribution. For the properties we wanted next, anti-melanogenic activity among them and several cosmetic functions we considered at the time, there is effectively no labelled data at all, and a model cannot be trained on an absence. The same scarcity explained both halves: where the field had data it also had models, and where it had no models it had no data either.

That diagnosis changed what the project was rather than how well it performed. If labels are scarce and tools are plentiful, the useful object is not another predictor but the layer that makes the existing ones answer a single question together. We stopped training and became an orchestrator — eighteen published scoring tools, each reproduced from its authors' code, arranged behind one funnel that turns a library of sequences into a ranked set of assemblable constructs. The decision was taken on evidence about the field rather than about our own model, and it is the reason everything downstream has the shape it has.

## A funnel is a cost model before it is a filter

Once eighteen tools sit in one pipeline, their ordering stops being cosmetic. Throughputs differ by close to two orders of magnitude — a composition-based classifier returns thousands of sequences per second, a protein language model tens — and on a pool of a million sequences that difference is measured in days rather than minutes. The funnel was therefore arranged by cost before it was arranged by logic: cheap and broad first, expensive and narrow last, on the smallest population that can still support a decision.

The clearest instance is the toxicity check. Run against the whole pool it was estimated at twenty-two hours against a budget of a few hours, which would have ended the project's first iteration by arithmetic. Moved behind the activity cut and applied to fifty thousand sequences it took forty-eight minutes and changed nothing in the result, because every sequence reaching it had already been selected on activity. The same reasoning later removed structure prediction from the platform stage entirely: the measurement was expensive, the confidence it returned on this material was low, and a number that cannot support a decision should not be bought at the largest population in the pipeline. A cost schedule of this kind is not an optimisation detail. It is a statement about which decisions are worth paying for, at what size, and in what order — and it is the difference between a design that finishes and one that is correct in principle and never completes.

## Combining scores forced a separation we had not planned

With eighteen tools behind one funnel, the obvious composition is a weighted total, and that is what the first complete run used. It worked. Just over a million sequences became a hundred and fifty assembled constructs in six and a half hours, and the output was coherent.

Reading those stored results as arithmetic then produced two findings, neither of which had announced itself as an error. The safety terms carried coefficients of 0.13 for toxicity, 0.09 for haemolysis and 0.05 for immunogenicity — each smaller than the activity weight they were supposed to override — so a peptide with a severe predicted haemolysis signal and a strong antioxidant score could still finish near the top of the table. And the composite used for ranking in the later rounds still contained the functional score, at the largest coefficient in it, which meant the control arm was being ordered partly by the property that defined it as the control. Nothing had failed. The run completed, the tables were complete, and the composition of the answer was wrong.

Two ideas replaced two coefficients. Whether a candidate is admissible is not a matter of degree, so it cannot live inside an average: it became a threshold, pass or eliminate, with no participation and no compensation. And a metric used to select must not be used again to rank, because if it is, the selection stops meaning anything: the functional score now appears exactly once, in the split between the two arms, and the final ranking is purely structural — surface exposure, aggregation propensity and structural confidence, and no functional term of any kind.

Both rules were later measured rather than assumed. In the antibacterial run, forty-seven per cent of the two hundred and fifty highest-scoring peptides were removed by the safety gates and the selected set fell to seven; a weighted design would have kept them and ranked them highly. The independence rule produced something that reads at first like a mistake — the eight highest-scoring constructs in the entire run come from the control arm. Structural quality and predicted activity turned out to be uncorrelated in this pool, which is precisely what two genuinely independent arms should reveal, and it changed the instruction we would give a wet lab: draw candidates from both.

## A score that cannot separate candidates is not evidence

The next defect was quieter and would not have been found by looking for failures. Several tools return values pinned into a narrow band across the whole pool. Their own documentation reports low confidence on this class of sequence, and in practice they carry almost no discriminating power — yet at a fixed weight they injected noise into every decision downstream of them, diluting the tools that could actually separate candidates. A constant is indistinguishable from a signal once it has been given a coefficient, and the damage is not to the tool's own column but to every column combined with it.

Weights therefore stopped being configured. Each component is normalised within the pool, oriented so that larger is better throughout, weighted by its winsorised standard deviation — the standard deviation after truncating extreme values to the fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles, so that a single outlier cannot dominate — and renormalised to one. A component with real spread is up-weighted automatically; a flat component approaches zero by construction. The mechanism then corrected something we had not planned for. When the epitope table turned out to be empty at run time, the weight computed for that component came out at exactly 0.00, against 0.31 for membrane crossing, 0.30 for allergenicity and 0.39 for aggregation, and the term vanished from the composite without anyone deciding to remove it. A configured weight would have spent a fifth of the score on a measurement that did not exist.

## The instrument, not the model, was the limit

The last phase was not building but auditing. Every tool was reproduced from its authors' code and model, its architecture and decision logic read closely, and several were retrained on better data in an attempt to improve them. The gains were marginal, and the same defects reappeared under a different representation — which locates the problem in the benchmark rather than in the model, and settles a question that would otherwise have been answered by training harder.

On the real library the reported accuracies did not survive the transfer. One functional predictor's published 0.5 boundary labelled 72.83 per cent of sequences positive; another labelled 99.5 per cent. A boundary that admits three-quarters of a library, or almost all of it, is not a gate, whatever accuracy it reported on its own test set. Functional selection moved from a probability threshold to an in-pool percentile, and the tools were reclassified by what each can actually support: three can eliminate a candidate, two can rank, one ranks weakly, and the rest only inform.

That reclassification is also why two of the four functional directions ship as pending signatures rather than as results. Where the only available model answers "does this sequence resemble a known functional peptide" rather than "does this suppress inflammation", the honest output is a ranked shortlist with the difference marked on every row and no pass-or-fail badge — not a result presented as equal to the two directions that can bear one.

## What the method buys, and what it does not

Three decisions make the output checkable rather than merely plausible. A control arm is carried through the whole pipeline under identical processing, so the result can be refuted and not only presented. Every sequence keeps its source identifier through every round, so any candidate's derivation can be reproduced from the stored record. And every threshold lives in exactly one place, because a convention held in two places drifts: an audit of an earlier version found the same threshold table written twice under a comment claiming the two shared a definition, and a route-specific immunogenicity threshold hard-coded globally, with the result that a construct sitting exactly on the tightened boundary was displayed as clearing every gate. The fix was structural — routes validate, services decide, repositories query, and the interface compares nothing — and the deployment now regenerates its data by calling those same service functions rather than re-deriving values, because an independent export path would have been a third copy of the same mistake and would have shipped silently.

One convention we insist on is negative, and it is the one most easily lost. A missing measurement is not a zero. Of the four hundred and ninety-six constructs, a hundred and ninety-eight carry no immunogenicity value from either pathway, and rendering those gaps as zeros would put two hundred candidates on screen described as immunogenic at zero and therefore safe, when none of them has been measured. The interface reports an indeterminate verdict instead. The distinction matters because safety conclusions are the part of this system that a reader is most likely to act on.

What the work does not establish is equally specific. No construct has been shown to be active. Every functional number is a prediction from a model trained on other data, and the wet-lab comparison that would test the premise of the whole design — that the two arms behave differently — is not in this document because it has not been run. The structural confidence across the dataset averages 0.42 on a scale where 0.50 marks the edge of meaningful confidence, which is a property of silk's glycine-alanine repeats rather than of the predictor, and the ranking built on those structures is a consistent way to order imperfect models, not a physical measurement. These are limits of the evidence, not of the method, and stating them is part of the method.

What we would claim instead is a way of deciding. A precondition is a threshold and never a weight. A score that cannot separate candidates is not evidence and should not receive a coefficient. A published boundary belongs to the population it was calibrated on and must be re-established on the one being screened, which is checkable before any biology is discussed. And where labelled data is scarce, the useful move is to change the question rather than to train harder. Each of these came from reading our own results rather than from a failure, and each changed where a decision lives rather than what value a parameter holds. That, more than any single number the pipeline produces, is the engineering we would want judged.
