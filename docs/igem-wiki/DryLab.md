# Dry Lab

## Aim

The dry-lab work exists to answer one question for each candidate molecule: which of the twenty million peptides in the library are worth taking into a lab, and in which product form. Everything else follows from that.

The question has a specific shape because of what is being designed. A recombinant fusion protein is three segments — a functional peptide, a scaffold protein, and a linker between them — and the segments are not independent. Changing the peptide changes the whole molecule's hydrophobicity and charge, and therefore its aggregation tendency. Changing the scaffold changes the chemical environment the peptide sits in, and therefore its apparent activity. Changing the linker changes how far the peptide stands off the scaffold surface, and therefore how accessible its active site stays. A candidate is therefore not a peptide with a score attached; it is a combination with a score attached, and the combination space is far too large to enumerate.

The aim is to reduce that space in a way that keeps the reasoning auditable. Four things are pre-computed and stored rather than computed on request: the peptide library with its per-tool prediction scores, the assembled constructs that survived selection, the scaffold reference library with its patent and regulatory evidence, and the linker reference library with its literature provenance. What the platform adds on top is a screening convention that the raw scores do not carry — a route-dependent safety profile, a route-dependent developability weighting, and a verdict structure that refuses to call an unmeasured gate a pass.

The scientific standard the work holds itself to is stated in one rule that recurs throughout: a prediction is not a measurement. Two of the four functional directions are marked work-in-progress because the models behind them do not currently support the claim they would have to support, and the platform reports them as ranked shortlists for wet-lab validation rather than as leads. That distinction is maintained in the data, in the API and in the interface, and it is the reason a wiki page for this project spends more space on limitations than on results.

## Design-Build-Test loop / Dry-wet loop

The dry-wet loop for this project is designed around the fact that the cheap experiment and the expensive experiment answer different questions, and only one of them is a question about activity.

**Build.** The library is built once: 20,248,885 peptides of 1 to 30 residues from ten sources, each carrying MD5 checksums and a source-version stamp, with scores from twelve predictors computed offline and stored in a 378-million-row table. Assembly is built on top of it: peptides are fused to scaffolds through linkers, and the resulting constructs are stored with their scores, their safety status and their channel. Both layers are immutable once computed, which is what makes the platform reproducible — nothing is recomputed at request time, so the same query returns the same number.

**Test (dry).** Testing happens in two places. The first is distributional: every predictor's score distribution over the whole library is examined, its separation between known-positive and known-negative reference sets is quantified with Cohen's d, and its behaviour is checked across peptide length and across source. The second is gate-level: a construct advances only if it clears every veto gate that was actually evaluated, and the pipeline's rule is that an unevaluated metric eliminates nothing. Both tests are cheap and both have changed decisions — a distributional check is what disqualified the tyrosinase predictor as a ranking signal, and a coverage check is what pushed B-cell epitope propensity to a zero weight in one direction.

**Test (wet).** The wet-lab loop is the only place where activity is measured, and it is designed around what the dry work cannot supply. The primary assays differ by direction because the claim differs by direction: radical-scavenging activity for the antioxidant direction, minimum inhibitory concentration for the antibacterial direction, NF-κB reporter inhibition for the anti-inflammatory direction, and tyrosinase inhibition for the anti-melanin direction. Around those sit the safety assays the computational gates are standing in for: haemolysis on red blood cells, T-cell proliferation for the immunogenicity routes, and skin irritation for the sensitisation signal. Each direction additionally carries the extra checks its route demands — sterility, biocompatibility and endotoxin control for wound dressings and injectable fillers, injectability and rheology for fillers, film formation and spreadability for topical films, wash resistance for hair coatings.

**Loop closure.** Two of the four directions cannot leave work-in-progress until a specific wet-lab result exists. The anti-inflammatory direction is blocked on a benchmark whose negatives must include experimentally-confirmed non-anti-inflammatory functional peptides, and the only way to obtain those is to generate them. The rebuilt tyrosinase model needs labels to calibrate against before its scores can be read as anything other than a ranking. Both loops therefore close in the same place: a small labelled experiment set, used either to retrain a model or to calibrate one.

The loop has one structural weakness that is worth stating rather than engineering around. The dry-lab gates are trained and calibrated on datasets that are not this project's own measurements, and the wet-lab work has not yet produced the first batch of labels that would let the thresholds be re-fitted on project data. Until it does, every threshold in the system is either a tool's own recommendation, a route convention chosen for physical reasons, or — in the case of toxicity — a calibration against this project's peptide distribution rather than against any experimental outcome.

## Pipeline

The pipeline is a funnel that narrows continuously, run once per direction. Four rounds are in scope; four more were designed and excluded, and the reason for exclusion is measured rather than budgetary.

**Round 1, functional ranking.** Each direction's functional predictor scores the full library and sorts it. The cut is not a fixed threshold but a percentile: the top 10% by score, roughly 2,024,889 peptides, becomes the candidate pool. The reason for a percentile rather than a probability boundary is that none of the functional predictors is calibrated well enough for a boundary to mean anything — one labels 72.83% of the library positive at 0.5, another labels 99.48% positive, and a third's entire distribution sits between 0.2 and 0.4. Alongside the top channel, a bottom channel is retained: the lowest-scoring peptides from the same ranking, kept as a baseline of known-poor activity so that later rounds can be checked for whether they discriminate. Both channels run the full downstream pipeline, so their numbers are comparable. This round is the largest computation but is not the bottleneck, at roughly half an hour because functional prediction is batch inference.

**Round 2, safety hard-threshold screening.** Three veto checks apply together and any one failure removes the candidate, with no weighted compensation. Toxicity below 0.38, haemolysis below 0.55, and MHC-I presentation under the route's gate. This round is the bottleneck: the toxicity predictor's inference is single-threaded and takes about twenty-four hours over twenty million peptides, and it cannot be interrupted. It is also the round that most clearly expresses the design position — passing is passing, failing is removal, and a strong functional score earns nothing here.

**Round 3, developability scoring.** Candidates that clear the gates are scored on developability and combined into a composite for ranking. Six components participate: cell penetration, solubility and thermal stability, each modulated by a route weight tier, plus allergenicity, B-cell epitope propensity and aggregation propensity, the last three entering inverted and at 1.0. Weights come from each component's Winsorized standard deviation within the pool, normalised to sum to one. A component with no measurement leaves the sum rather than entering as a zero, and the remaining weights are renormalised. This round takes about five hours. One principle governs it: each metric is used exactly once. The functional score appears in round 1 and nowhere after, because a functional score that keeps entering the weighting will eventually select for the highest functional score and bury the scaffold-and-route fit that the later rounds exist to establish.

**Round 4, enumeration and assembly.** Ranked peptides are fused to scaffolds through linkers and stored as complete constructs. This is where a peptide becomes a molecule that could go into a lab, and it is the origin of the 496 constructs. One implementation gap lives here: the scaffold segment was written as a placeholder rather than a real binding, so the assembly script stored the minimal viable combination to make the table structure and example data complete, leaving real binding to the compute service.

**Rounds 5 to 8, excluded.** The original design had four more rounds, all concerning three-dimensional structure: structure prediction by a general folding model, structure-based evaluation of solvent-accessible surface area and three-dimensional aggregation, a structure-based ranking, and high-accuracy validation of a small number of selected constructs. The failure is specific and measured. Silk fibroin sequences are built from short glycine-glycine-any-residue repeats with no natural structural template, and general folding predictors return a mean pLDDT of about 0.42 on them, where 1.00 is full confidence and above 0.70 is generally required before a structure supports a decision. Solvent exposure and aggregation computed from a structure at that confidence have no decision value, so rounds 5 to 8 leave scope together. The choice concentrates resources on making the sequence-level work solid rather than displaying a structure that cannot be used.

Three things in that table are worth reading carefully. The antibacterial direction retained 7 of 150 top candidates, which is not a pipeline failure but a consequence of its ranker: 47% of that model's top 250 peptides are toxic and 80% are haemolytic, because the model is good at exactly the cationic amphipathic peptides that disrupt membranes. The anti-melanin direction ran on the full library with no pre-filter because its only available predictor has no enrichment power, so the top and bottom channels were taken directly by percentile slicing; the resulting asymmetry between channels, 83 against 24, reflects the safety gate acting differently on compositionally unusual low-scoring peptides rather than any difference in depigmenting activity. The wall-clock figures are the selection, gating, scoring and assembly queries against the stored scores, not the predictor inference, which happened once per predictor over the full library and is what the half-hour and twenty-four-hour figures in rounds 1 and 2 refer to.

## Math Models

### E. coli expression toxicity

Expression in E. coli is where a construct that scores well on activity and safety can still fail, because a protein that expresses as an insoluble inclusion body has no useful activity and no useful yield regardless of what its functional peptide does. Inclusion-body formation is the dominant failure mode of recombinant expression, and the pipeline treats predicting it as a first-class part of developability rather than as an afterthought.

The model used is a soluble-expression predictor whose semantic target is exactly this: whether a protein remains soluble when recombinantly expressed in E. coli. On its native target — proteins of the length actually expressed in E. coli — it reaches an AUC of approximately 0.71, which is useful but not strong. Its behaviour on this project's short peptides is a different matter, and the reason is worth stating precisely because it is a general caution about machine-learned scores. Applied to the twenty-million-peptide library it achieves an AUC of 1.0, which looks like a perfect result and is actually circular: the predictor's training labels were themselves generated by applying a threshold to the predictor's own output, so the evaluation reproduces the labelling function rather than testing it. The semantic mismatch compounds it — "soluble when recombinantly expressed" is not "dissolves in water", and for a free peptide the two are different questions with different answers.

The pipeline's response is to place the model at the level where its semantics apply. Thermal stability and soluble expression are both computed on the assembled fusion protein — scaffold of several hundred residues, plus linker, plus peptide — rather than on the peptide alone, and both are labelled advisory when they are reported, because they answer a question about the whole molecule rather than about the functional segment. On peptides of 3 to 30 residues the thermal-stability predictor is not used at all: its shortest training sequences are several hundred residues, and on short peptides its output collapses to a single peak near 0.5 with no biological signal, while on full-length proteins it reaches a Matthews correlation of 0.60 to 0.91.

Aggregation is handled separately and at peptide level, because the failure it predicts is visible earlier. A rule-based method assigns each amino acid an intrinsic aggregation propensity on a residue scale and accumulates it over a sliding window, producing an estimate of how likely the whole sequence is to form amyloid or non-specific aggregates; values above 22 are called aggregation-prone. This is the one developability signal computed on the peptide itself rather than on the construct, and it is placed in the enumeration stage as a pre-filter, because aggregation is the most common cause of failure at the expression-and-purification step and a candidate that will form inclusion bodies should never reach the wet lab. The three-dimensional alternative, which requires a predicted structure and a residue-scale three-dimensional propensity surface, is out of scope for the reason given above: the structures are not reliable enough on these sequences.

The honest summary of this section is that expression-related risk is modelled at two levels of confidence. Peptide-level aggregation propensity is a rule-based estimate that can be wrong in either direction but has no learned bias. Construct-level solubility and thermal stability are learned models applied where their training distribution matches the question, carrying an advisory label and an explicit note that their short-peptide behaviour is uninformative. Neither is a wet-lab result, and the loop closes only when the first expressed construct is assayed for soluble yield.

### Molecular docking

No molecular docking has been performed in this project, and the reason is a measured failure upstream of it rather than a choice to prioritise other work.

Docking requires a three-dimensional structure of the target. For the anti-melanin direction the target is tyrosinase, which has published crystal structures and would be a reasonable candidate for docking study in isolation. For the other three directions the targets are reactive oxygen species, bacterial membranes and inflammatory signalling pathways, none of which is a single dockable receptor in the way a small-molecule enzyme is. The platform's design does not treat these as receptor-binding events: the functional peptide's activity is not modelled as occupying a binding pocket, and there is no structural hypothesis in the pipeline for it to dock against.

The scaffold side of the question is the one that would have needed structural work, and that is where the measurement blocks it. The silk and collagen sequences in the library are built from short repeating motifs — glycine-serine-rich repeats in fibroin, glycine-proline-hydroxyproline repeats in collagen — that have no natural structural template. General-purpose folding predictors return a mean pLDDT of about 0.42 on these sequences, where 1.00 is perfect confidence and above 0.70 is the usual threshold for treating a predicted structure as supporting a decision. A docking calculation performed on a structure at that confidence would produce a number, and the number would not mean anything. The same measurement is what excluded the four structure-dependent pipeline rounds.

Docking remains a reasonable thing to want. The form it would take here is not peptide-against-receptor but peptide-against-motif: whether a functional peptide can be positioned on a collagen or fibroin surface without occluding the motif the scaffold is supposed to form, which is a question about steric accessibility of the functional group rather than about a binding affinity. Answering it needs a structure for the fusion construct, which needs either a designed scaffold with a known fold or experimental structural data on the construct. Both are downstream of the wet-lab work, and neither is in the current version. The node is retained in the wiki structure as a declared intention with its precondition stated, rather than filled with a result that does not exist.

### Delivery

Delivery here means the route a finished product takes to reach the tissue it acts on, and it is modelled as the physical quantity it actually is: how intact the barrier is between the construct and the immune system. This is the one place in the platform where a product decision and a biological quantity are deliberately identified with each other, and the identification is what makes the screening profiles derivable rather than arbitrary.

Five routes are defined, and they are ordered by barrier integrity rather than by application name. Into tissue — an injectable filler delivered through a needle into dermis or subcutaneous tissue — bypasses the barrier entirely and is the only route that genuinely enters the body. Breached barrier — a wound dressing on an open wound bed, in direct contact with tissue fluid. Intact barrier under occlusion — a mask or patch, where occlusion raises stratum corneum permeability and therefore lifts penetration and sensitisation risk together. Intact barrier without occlusion — a topical film spread on the skin that dries down to a protein film, with the construct largely outside the stratum corneum. Non-living surface — a hair coating applied to the hair shaft, which is keratinised dead tissue with no blood vessels, lymphatics or immune cells, so the construct never enters the body at all.

The immunogenicity gate follows this ordering directly: 0.35 for the breached-barrier and into-tissue routes, 0.50 for the two intact-barrier routes, and 0.60 for the non-living surface. The tightened value for wound dressings is the one that needs justification, and the justification is that a breached barrier puts the construct in direct contact with tissue fluid, which places immune exposure in the same band as injection; treating an open wound as intact skin understates the risk.

Developability weights follow the same physical picture rather than being assigned. A topical film is used at room temperature, so thermal stability matters least and receives the low tier at 0.4; with neither a needle nor occlusion to drive penetration, membrane penetration becomes the binding constraint and receives the high tier at 1.8; spreading into a film demands solubility and also receives 1.8. A mask patch relies on occlusion, so its penetration requirement drops to the medium tier while film and impregnation processing keep solubility high. An injectable filler goes straight into tissue, so penetration is unnecessary and takes the low tier while solubility and thermal stability sit at medium. A hair coating is applied to dead keratinised fibre, penetration matters least, and solubility is required at the high tier to survive a shampoo formulation.

Two further route-level decisions are worth recording. Application technique — manual spreading, spray, needle injection, shampoo formulation — carries no screening weight and filters nothing; it was demoted from a user-facing choice to descriptive text because a mandatory choice that changes no outcome is a choice that conveys nothing. And each route carries a set of non-vetoing extra checks specific to its dosage form: sterility, biocompatibility and endotoxin control for wound dressings; sterility, endotoxin control, injectability, rheology and in vivo safety for fillers; irritation and sensitisation under occlusion for patches; film formation and spreadability for topical films; adhesion to the shaft, wash resistance and no adverse effect on hair feel for hair coatings. These are not in the data and not scored. They are the experiment list a route obliges, presented as a prompt rather than as a verdict.

## Results

**Library and coverage.** The library holds 20,248,885 peptides across ten sources, with the metagenomic source contributing 94.6% of rows. Twelve predictors cover the full library; the two immunogenicity predictors cover partially because they are length-restricted, which is a property of the biology rather than of the run. The two largest result tables hold roughly 378 million rows and 130 GB, and 147.5 million rows and 35 GB respectively.

**Construct yield.** 496 constructs survived from 20,248,885 peptides, distributed 170, 65, 154 and 107 across the four directions with both top and bottom channels populated in each. The elimination rate is above 99.99%, and the four directions retain very different numbers because their rankers differ in dynamic range rather than because any of them was run less completely.

**Distributional separation.** Cohen's d between known-positive and known-negative reference sets is extreme for every tool that was measured on a reference set, ranging from 2.61 for the allergenicity predictor through 3.28 and 3.29 for the toxicity and haemolysis predictors, 4.12 and 4.34 for the antibacterial classifier and the antioxidant predictor, 7.19 for cell penetration, and 14.2 to 37.0 for the two immunogenicity predictors. Extreme separation on a curated reference set and useless discrimination on the real library are not in conflict — they measure different things, and the gap between them is the reason functional scores are used for ranking and never as gates.

**Coverage-driven findings.** Three results came out of coverage analysis rather than from modelling. The B-cell epitope predictor was absent when the antioxidant direction ran, and the data-driven weighting assigned it a weight of exactly zero, which is correct behaviour and also means that direction's composite contains no epitope contribution — a fact about which tools were loaded, not about the chemistry. The MHC-II predictor was integrated from a separate 147.5-million-row table for three of the four directions, raising assessed coverage on that pathway from zero to complete for those directions. The haemolysis predictor, sampled at 500,000 peptides when the first direction ran, has since been extended to full coverage.

**Per-round timing.** Functional ranking is about thirty minutes; safety screening is about twenty-four hours and is the bottleneck, dominated by single-threaded toxicity inference; developability scoring is about five hours; enumeration and assembly is about thirty minutes. The per-direction selection and assembly queries that produced the stored results run in seconds.

**What is not in the results.** Two directions are work-in-progress and their numbers are ranked shortlists rather than leads: the anti-inflammatory direction because both of its better models are absent from the production database and the one remaining model has a known training leak, and the anti-melanin direction because its only available predictor labels 99.48% of the library positive and the rebuilt replacement is not deployed. The MHC-I gate is unassessed for 379 of 496 constructs, both immunogenicity pathways are unassessed for 198, and the pipeline's rule that an unassessed gate eliminates nothing means those constructs' safety case rests on toxicity, haemolysis and developability alone. No molecular docking, no three-dimensional modelling, and no wet-lab measurement has contributed to any number on the platform.
